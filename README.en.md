## Purpose and scope

This repository provides a rigorous, accessible introduction to China's artificial-intelligence and robotics ecosystem. It does not treat China as a single technological actor. Instead, it distinguishes among:

- central-government priorities and policy instruments;
- provincial and municipal implementation;
- academic and corporate research;
- markets for models, semiconductors, vehicles, and robots;
- verified industrial adoption versus product announcements;
- formal regulation, enforcement practice, and social impact.

The phrase “Chinese AI” covers organisations with markedly different incentives: ministries, local governments, universities, state-owned enterprises, digital platforms, manufacturers, and start-ups. Claims in this repository therefore identify the relevant actor, period, and source whenever the evidence permits.

## Five points that frame the current landscape

### 1. China does not lead every dimension of AI in the same way

China leads in AI publication volume, citations, and patent grants, while the United States continues to produce more models classified as notable. The performance gap between leading American and Chinese systems narrowed quickly during 2024 and 2025. This combination—scientific scale, intense commercial competition, and constrained access to advanced compute—describes the landscape more accurately than a single winner-versus-loser ranking.

Source: [Stanford AI Index 2026](https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2026-ai-index-report/research-and-development).

### 2. Industrial robotics is China's clearest scale advantage

According to the International Federation of Robotics, China installed about 354,000 industrial robots in 2025, or 59% of global installations, and Chinese suppliers held 55% of the domestic market. The operational stock of 2.027 million refers to 2024 and remains a historical observation.

These figures concern installed industrial robots. They should not be conflated with humanoid-robot sales or with general “physical AI” capability.

Source: [IFR, World Robotics 2026](https://ifr.org/downloads/press_docs/EN-2026-SEP-24-IFR_Press_Release_WR-CHINA.pdf).

### 3. Policy has shifted from capacity building towards widespread deployment

The 2017 New Generation Artificial Intelligence Development Plan established a roadmap to 2030. The 14th Five-Year Plan reinforced AI's status as a strategic technology. In August 2025, the State Council's “AI Plus” initiative deepened the emphasis on adoption across science, industry, consumption, public welfare, governance, and international cooperation.

The 2017 plan's target of roughly RMB 1 trillion for the core AI industry by 2030 is an industry-value target, not public expenditure or government investment already committed. This distinction corrects a frequent misreading of the corresponding “USD 150 billion” figure.

Source: [State Council, AI Plus initiative](https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/content/202508/content_7037861.htm).

### 4. Chinese AI regulation is layered rather than monolithic

China does not have one comprehensive “AI law.” The relevant framework combines the Cybersecurity Law, Data Security Law, Personal Information Protection Law (PIPL), algorithmic-recommendation rules, deep-synthesis provisions, interim measures for generative-AI services, and rules for labelling AI-generated or synthetic content.

The generative-AI measures apply to services offered to the public in China. Among other duties, they address personal-information protection, content governance, complaint mechanisms, and—in specified circumstances—security assessment and algorithm filing.

Sources: [CAC interim measures for generative AI](https://www.cac.gov.cn/2023-07/13/c_1690898327029107.htm) and [CAC rules on AI-generated content labelling](https://www.cac.gov.cn/2025-03/14/c_1743654684782215.htm).

### 5. “Social credit” is not a single national citizen score

China's social-credit framework comprises regulatory records, compliance mechanisms, redlists and blacklists, sector-specific arrangements, and local experiments. It does not assign one comprehensive national score to every citizen. That clarification does not remove legitimate concerns about due process, privacy, surveillance, or proportionality; it allows those concerns to be examined more precisely.

Sources: [2014 State Council planning outline](https://www.ndrc.gov.cn/fzggw/jgsj/cjd/sjdt/201406/t20140627_1111342.html) and [Jiang, 2020](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/2057047320959856).

## Ecosystem map

### Models and platforms

- **Alibaba Cloud — Qwen:** a multilingual family of language and multimodal models supported by an international open-weight strategy.
- **DeepSeek:** a research company known for reasoning-oriented systems and for releasing technical reports and weights for several models.
- **Baidu — ERNIE:** foundation models and services integrated with Baidu's cloud and product ecosystem.
- **Zhipu AI — GLM:** foundation models, agents, and enterprise services.
- **Moonshot AI — Kimi:** assistants and models with an emphasis on long-context and consumer applications.
- **MiniMax:** text, speech, music, and video models.
- **ByteDance — Doubao/Seed:** models and products backed by large-scale consumer platforms.
- **Huawei — Pangu and Ascend:** sectoral models, computing infrastructure, and AI accelerators.

Read [Companies and model ecosystems](docs/en/companies-models.md).

### Robotics and embodied AI

- **Unitree:** quadruped and humanoid robots, including the H1 and G1.
- **UBTECH:** Walker humanoids for industrial and service settings.
- **AgiBot/Zhiyuan:** humanoids, manipulation, and robot-data platforms.
- **Fourier Intelligence:** rehabilitation robotics and GR-series humanoids.
- **XPENG Robotics:** the IRON humanoid; XPENG's aerial-mobility activity belongs to AEROHT/ARIDGE, not to a product called “ALEVEN.”
- **DJI and XAG:** aerial robotics for imaging, inspection, and agriculture.
- **SIASUN, Estun, and Inovance:** industrial automation, controls, and components.

Read [Robotics and industry](docs/en/robotics-industry.md).

## Priority application domains

1. **Manufacturing:** welding, assembly, visual inspection, intralogistics, maintenance, and quality control.
2. **Mobility:** advanced driver assistance, automated driving, robotaxis, vehicle–road cooperation, and autonomous logistics.
3. **Healthcare:** diagnostic support, drug discovery, hospital operations, and surgical or rehabilitation robotics.
4. **Commerce and logistics:** recommendation, customer service, content generation, warehousing, and last-mile delivery.
5. **Science:** molecular prediction, materials, climate, engineering, and laboratory automation.
6. **Government and cities:** traffic management, public services, security, and administration—uses whose effectiveness, rights implications, and accountability mechanisms require separate analysis.

Read [Applications, ethics, and society](docs/en/applications-ethics.md).
